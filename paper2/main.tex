\begin{abstract}
\noindent
General-purpose language models answer questions about plants fluently, and people
increasingly ask them. For the regional floras of the tropics --- the long tail of
plant diversity --- it is not known how often those answers are true, nor whether the
published record is even able to settle them. We assemble a verifiable test bed for
Costa Rica: a structured catalogue of \textbf{6{,}946} species extracted from the
\emph{Manual de Plantas de Costa Rica}, \textbf{999{,}625} cleaned national occurrence
records from a frozen, citable GBIF download, and derived evidence linking species to
\textbf{143} protected areas, botanical regions and elevation. On that base we build a
benchmark of everyday questions --- where a species grows, at what elevation, when it
flowers, whether it can be grown in a given place, what it is called --- in which every
question carries a structured answer key together with the \emph{level} of evidence
behind it: stated by the flora, inferred from occurrence data, inferable only from
relatives, or absent from the record. Answers are scored twice: the direct response
against the key, and every individual claim against the species' full record, with
\emph{contradicted} kept strictly apart from \emph{not in the record}. We compare a
closed-book frontier model, the same model with web search, a local open-weights model,
and a grounded system that answers only from this evidence and abstains when the record
is silent. We report factual precision, contradiction rate, appropriate abstention and
coverage, and we examine how each varies along two axes that we show are only weakly
related: how well known a species is globally, and how well recorded it is locally.

\section{Introduction}

Anyone with a phone now asks a language model where a plant grows, when it flowers, or
whether it can be planted on their farm. The answers are fluent and specific. Whether
they are true is a separate question, and for most of the world's plant diversity it is
an open one: training data is dominated by temperate, well-studied, well-photographed
taxa, while a tropical regional flora consists largely of species that are rarely
collected and seldom written about outside a single published treatment.

Two things follow, and this paper separates them deliberately.

\paragraph{Are the answers true?} Model accuracy is known to decay with the popularity
of the entity in question \citep{kandpal2023longtail,mallen2023popqa}. For organisms
this has been measured mainly closed-book and at global scale
\citep{dorm2025ecological}. What a regional user asks --- in Spanish, about a species
with eleven herbarium records --- is the hardest case and the least measured.

\paragraph{Can they be answered at all?} A question is only answerable if some source
settles it. For a flora this is testable: the \emph{Manual de Plantas de Costa Rica}
states elevation, slope, region and flowering months for most of its species; it rarely
states whether a plant wants sun or shade, and it almost never states uses. Occurrence
records can settle some of what the text does not, and nothing settles the rest. Which
everyday questions fall in which class is itself a result, and it is the kind of
information-completeness analysis published as a biodiversity diagnosis
\citep{arvizu2025reptiles}.

Keeping the two apart matters because they are usually confounded. A system that
answers everything looks better than one that abstains, until abstention is counted as
the correct behaviour where the record is silent. Our evaluation therefore scores
\emph{what the record supports}, reports what no source can verify as its own category,
and never treats silence as falsehood.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{verifiable test bed} for a tropical regional flora: structured
        catalogue, cleaned national occurrence evidence, protected-area and region
        linkage, and a federated common-name layer, all reproducible from citable
        sources (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item A \textbf{benchmark of everyday questions with typed answer keys}, sampled
        along two orthogonal popularity axes, in which each key records its evidence
        level and its provenance (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}).
  \item A \textbf{two-level verification protocol} that scores the direct answer and
        each individual claim, separating contradiction from absence of evidence, with
        the checker itself calibrated against hand-labelled answers
        (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).
  \item An \textbf{empirical comparison} of closed-book, web-augmented, local and
        grounded systems along the long tail, reporting precision together with
        coverage rather than accuracy alone (Section~\ref{sec:systems}).
  \item A \textbf{diagnosis of answerability}: for each everyday question type, what
        fraction of the flora the published record can settle, and by what evidence
        (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Ecological knowledge in language models.} \citet{dorm2025ecological}
evaluate frontier models on ecological tasks --- species presence, range maps, threat
classification, trait estimation --- and find measurable but uneven knowledge. That work
is closed-book and global; we test a single regional flora, compare grounded against
ungrounded answering, and verify claim by claim against a published source.

\paragraph{Retrieval grounding.} Retrieval-augmented generation \citep{lewis2020rag}
improves factuality most for less popular entities \citep{mallen2023popqa}, and
retrieval generally outperforms fine-tuning for injecting specific knowledge
\citep{ovadia2024finetuning}. Our own earlier system \citep{araya2026grounding} showed
that supplying the retrieved flora entry raises elevation agreement substantially for
Costa Rican plants; it evaluated grounded answers against the same flora, which bounds
what that result can claim. The present work changes the target: questions whose keys
come from occurrence data, from experts, or from nothing at all.

\paragraph{Extraction from biodiversity text.} Recent tools apply language models to
pull occurrence and trait data out of literature, with explicit hallucination checks
\citep{arete2025}. That line extracts \emph{into} the record; we evaluate answers
\emph{out of} it.

\paragraph{Mobilizing what is already written down.} Much of what a regional user
wants to know exists only inside documents, and recovering it is an established line of
work: data are rescued from environmental assessment reports that were never meant as
occurrence sources \citep{telletxea2026stray}, vague textual localities such as
``near'' are interpreted into usable geography \citep{campbell2024near}, and traits
are derived automatically and anchored back to the specimens that justify them
\citep{bakis2026raunkiaeran}. Our target is the step after mobilization: once the
record is structured, which everyday questions can it actually answer, and does a
language model answer them truthfully.

\paragraph{Claim-level verification.} Decomposing generated text into atomic claims and
checking each against a source \citep{min2023factscore}, and separating truthfulness
from informativeness \citep{lin2022truthfulqa}, are standard in NLP evaluation but have
not been applied to a regional flora with a domain record as the authority.

\subsection{Catalogue}
Species entries were segmented from the OCR'd volumes and parsed into structured fields
--- elevation range, slope, botanical regions, forest types, growth form, flowering
months, endemism --- together with the verbatim distribution paragraph, morphological
description and taxonomic discussion. The structured fields are produced
deterministically, so each value can be traced to the sentence that produced it; an
automated audit found \textbf{100\%} of extracted elevation, slope and endemism values
to be literally supported by their source paragraph \pending{cite the companion paper
or restate the audit here}.

\subsection{Occurrence evidence}
Records were cleaned with the rules used by the validated mapping pipeline: accepted
taxon keys only, coordinates inside the national bounding box, swapped latitude and
longitude repaired, records coarser than 10~km discarded. Each record carries elevation
sampled from a digital elevation model, its botanical region, and a 30~arc-second cell
identifier used to count distinct collection sites rather than duplicate records. Per
species we aggregate record counts, distinct sites, elevation quantiles, regions,
record months, and the protected areas the records fall within.

\subsection{Common names}\label{sec:names}
Everyday questions use everyday names, which are ambiguous by nature. Names are
federated from the flora itself, from the name iNaturalist marks as preferred for Costa
Rica, and from the global indexes of GBIF and Tropicos, each row retaining its source in
the shape of the Darwin Core vernacular-name extension. Resolution returns ranked
candidates and an explicit decision rather than a single species: in our data
\emph{cortez amarillo} denotes three species of \emph{Handroanthus} and \emph{poró}
denotes ten species of \emph{Erythrina}. Global indexes raise coverage and lower local
precision --- \emph{nazareno} returns a species used by that name elsewhere in Latin
America --- so sources are ranked, with country-specific sources above global ones.

\section{Benchmark}\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Species sampling}
Two notions of ``rare'' are usually conflated. A species may be globally familiar and
locally scarce (\emph{Stellaria media}: 821{,}441 records worldwide, 2 in Costa Rica) or
locally well collected and globally obscure. Over our frame the two measures correlate
only moderately (Spearman $\rho = 0.46$, $n = 6{,}917$), and they drive different
hypotheses: global familiarity should govern what a model knows, local evidence governs
what the record can confirm. We therefore stratify on both, in a $3 \times 3$ grid of
tertiles, with equal allocation per cell so that the off-diagonal cells are as well
represented as the diagonal. The frame excludes unnamed morphospecies and species whose
accepted key is shared with another catalogue entry, since their occurrence evidence
cannot be attributed. Pilot ($n = 18$) and main ($n = 99$) samples are disjoint and
frozen with a recorded seed; prompts and rubrics are tuned only on the pilot.

\subsection{Question types and answer keys}
Questions are posed in Spanish, in the form a visitor, grower or naturalist would use.
Each carries a structured key, the source of that key, and an evidence level
(Table~\ref{tab:questions}). Place questions are generated in three varieties ---
confirmed by records, possible by habitat overlap, and unlikely --- so that a yes/no key
is never trivially ``yes''.

The pilot comprises 190 questions over 18 species and the main set 1{,}020 over 99
species. Keys are explicitly scoped to \emph{what the record supports}: for a
cosmopolitan weed collected once in the country, a key may be right about the record and
wrong about the field. Keys asserting absence for species that are globally familiar but
locally sparse are flagged for expert review, since that is where the record is most
likely incomplete.

\section{Systems compared}\label{sec:systems}

The comparison is a two-factor design --- generator crossed with evidence --- rather
than a list of named systems, so that any two cells differ in exactly one respect
(Table~\ref{tab:design}).

\begin{description}
  \item[A1, A2] Closed-book frontier models. What a model knows unaided; two vendors so
    the result is not a property of one system.
  \item[B] Frontier model with web search --- what a person actually gets today, and the
    only arm whose evidence we do not control. Retrieved URLs are logged, because the
    flora and the occurrence data are themselves online.
  \item[C] Local open-weights model, closed book: a reproducible floor that needs no
    proprietary service.
  \item[D] The grounded system. Answers are composed only from the evidence pack
    assembled for the question, every claim carries its source, and the system abstains
    where the record is silent.
  \item[E] Ablation: the same generator and prompt over the flora text alone. The
    contrast D\,--\,E isolates what the occurrence, protected-area, region and terrain
    evidence contributes beyond the written treatment.
  \item[F] The local model over the same evidence pack. The contrast F\,--\,D asks
    whether grounding lets hardware a regional institution can afford match a frontier
    service.
  \item[G] A template answerer with no language model, composing the same evidence
    deterministically. It bounds how much of the benchmark requires generation at all.
\end{description}

Every model arm receives one prompt template in which only the evidence block differs,
including an identical statement that the system may decline to answer. Granting
abstention to the grounded arm alone would make the abstention comparison meaningless.
All arms see the same questions in the same order at temperature zero; run-to-run
variability is reported separately, from replicates on a fixed balanced subset.

\section{Evaluation framework}\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Two levels}
Each answer is scored on the \emph{direct response} against the question's key, and on
\emph{every factual claim it makes along the way} against the species' full record. A
language model only decomposes the answer into typed claims and reads its stance; all
comparisons --- elevations, months, slopes, growth forms, places --- are made by
deterministic parsers against stored values, so the scoring itself is not a model
judgement.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Verdict vocabulary. Absence of evidence is never scored as falsehood.}
\label{tab:verdicts}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llp{8.6cm}}
\toprule
Level & Verdict & Meaning \\
\midrule
Direct & correct              & matches the key within tolerance \\
       & partially correct    & overlaps but overstates or understates the range \\
       & overstated           & asserts more certainty than the record supports \\
       & understated          & hedges where the record is definite \\
       & wrong                & contradicts the key \\
       & abstained            & declines; correct where the record is silent \\
       & beyond record        & asserts something no source covers; expert adjudicates \\
\midrule
Claim  & supported            & the record states it, or records show it \\
       & contradicted         & the record states otherwise \\
       & not in the record    & checkable, but the record does not contain it \\
       & unverifiable         & no source covers this kind of claim \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The distinction between \emph{contradicted} and \emph{not in the record} carries the
scientific weight. Occurrence data reflects collection effort, so absence of records is
not absence of the species, and a claim the flora does not make is not thereby false.
Likewise, a true statement the flora happens not to contain --- a widely known use of a
pasture grass, say --- is routed to \emph{beyond record} for expert adjudication rather
than counted as an error.

\subsection{Metrics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Factual precision}: supported $/$ (supported $+$ contradicted).
  \item \textbf{Contradiction rate}: the share of answers containing a claim the record
        refutes --- confident falsehood, the quantity that matters to a user.
  \item \textbf{Unverifiable rate}: claims no source can check; the surface on which
        confabulation is invisible to automated scoring.
  \item \textbf{Abstention quality}: abstaining where the record is silent, and
        answering where it is not.
  \item \textbf{Informativeness}: whether a question was answered at all, so that
        systems cannot win by refusing.
  \item \textbf{Risk--coverage}: precision as a function of the share of questions
        answered, which is the only fair comparison between a system that always
        answers and one that may decline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Validating the checker}
The checker is calibrated on hand-written answers with known verdicts spanning correct,
wrong, overextended, overconfident, hedged, abstaining and invented responses; it
currently reproduces \textbf{24/24} direct verdicts and detects \textbf{12/12} planted
false claims. Those answers are author-written and cleaner than real model output, so
before the main run a sample of \pending{$n$} real answers is hand-labelled and
agreement with the checker reported.

\subsection{Expert involvement}
Experts contribute at three points: labelling light requirements, for which no record
source exists; reviewing flagged answer keys where the record is likely incomplete; and
adjudicating claims marked \emph{beyond record}. A subset of answers is additionally
ranked blind for trustworthiness, with overlapping items across experts so that
agreement can be reported.

\section{Planned analyses}\label{sec:analysis}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Truthfulness along the long tail.} Factual precision and contradiction
        rate as functions of global familiarity and local evidence, with species as a
        random effect. Hypothesis: closed-book precision falls with global familiarity;
        grounded precision does not.
  \item \textbf{Does grounding help, and where?} Grounded versus closed-book and versus
        web search, by question type. Hypothesis: the gain concentrates in the
        locally-sparse cells, where the web has least to offer.
  \item \textbf{Answerability diagnosis.} For each question type, the share of the
        flora the record can settle and at what evidence level --- the biodiversity
        diagnosis component.
  \item \textbf{The cost of abstention.} Risk--coverage curves per system.
  \item \textbf{Where keys and reality diverge.} Expert adjudication of flagged keys,
        quantifying how often the published record is incomplete for everyday
        questions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to validity}

\textbf{The key is the record, not the world.} Scoring against a flora measures
agreement with a published treatment. We mitigate by scoping every key explicitly, by
flagging the cells where the record is most likely incomplete, and by expert
adjudication. \textbf{Occurrence bias.} Records reflect collection effort; we never
derive absence from them and report counts as effort. \textbf{Checker error.} The
decomposition step is a model, with the measured calibration above and a planned
human-labelled sample. \textbf{Web-search non-determinism.} Search-augmented answers
depend on retrieval date; dates and model versions are recorded with every answer.
\textbf{Scope.} Volumes VII--VIII are not yet in the catalogue, so a few common genera
(for example \emph{Cecropia}) are absent; this is reported rather than hidden.
